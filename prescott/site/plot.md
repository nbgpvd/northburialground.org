# The Prescott Post lot

You are standing in a burial lot of the Grand Army of the Republic, the organization of Union veterans. The monument at its centre reads "Prescott Post, No. 1, G.A.R., A.D. 1875." [1] Around it stand about three hundred small white government headstones in rows. [2] The earliest death carved on them is 1876; the latest are in the 1930s. [2]

## How the lot began

In 1871 Prescott Post asked the City of Providence for a burial lot here. On December 21, 1871, the City Council granted it. The first condition was that the lot be deeded to Prescott Post "for the use of all the Posts of the G. A. R. in this city, as a burial place for the comrades of their several Posts." [3]

One of those posts was Ives Post No. 13, a post of Black veterans, chartered in 1868. [4] The grant does not mention race. It names every post, and Ives Post was one of them. [3][4]

The Post dedicated the monument on Decoration Day, Saturday, May 29, 1875. Its shaft is an old 32-pounder cannon, ten and a half feet long, that Congress voted to the Post. It stands on a base of Westerly granite. [3]

## Who is here

Every stone marks a man who served the Union in the Civil War: in regiments from Rhode Island, Massachusetts, Connecticut, Maine, New York, Pennsylvania and a dozen other states, in the regular army, and in the Navy. [2] Among them are men of the 14th Rhode Island Heavy Artillery (Colored), the state's Black regiment, and of the 55th Massachusetts, the 29th Connecticut, and more than a dozen regiments of United States Colored Troops. [2] By June 2025, fifty-two of the men buried here had been identified as Black veterans. [5]

They lie in the same rows. A corporal of the 14th Rhode Island is next to a sailor; a private of the 1st U.S. Colored Infantry is next to a man of the 2nd Rhode Island. [2] The stones themselves say nothing about race. They give a name, a company, a regiment, and a date of death. [2]

## What the City did

The City kept its own burial grounds differently. In 1883 its free burials, for those whose families could not pay, ran in two numbered series: a White Free Ground and a Colored Free Ground. [6]

In 1885 the General Assembly passed a law: honorably discharged soldiers and sailors buried in pauper ground had to be moved out of it. [7] The City's committee on the North Burial Ground found this lot too small to take them. In June 1886 the City Council voted to enlarge it by annexing the Providence Police Association's lot, and to move the monument to the centre of the larger lot. [7]

Zebedee Howland, sergeant major of the 14th Rhode Island, had died in March 1883. The City buried him in the Colored Free Ground. On September 8, 1886, ten weeks after the City authorized the enlargement, his body was raised and reburied here, at the City's charge. The clerk struck the old entry and wrote "Soldier" beside it. [6] His stone is at G3.

Howland's is the one case documented so far. The 1885 law makes it likely there were others, Black and white. The City's burial ledgers will show how many.

## How the rows were filled

The dates on the stones run in order. Deaths of the 1870s and 1880s are in the rows nearest the monument. From there the dates run outward in both directions, a row at a time: the 1890s, the 1900s, the 1910s, and in the rows nearest the road the 1920s and 1930s. [2] From the 1890s on, the Post buried its men in the order they died, each in the next grave.

The oldest rows are less simple. The lot was enlarged and its monument re-centred after 1886, and men who had died years earlier were brought in that year. Where a man in those rows lies may depend on when he was reburied, not when he died. Nothing in the record found so far shows anyone was placed by race. The Post's own records, if they survive, are where that will be settled.

## A caution

The men here are buried together. That does not mean they served together. Each stone's regiment is what the stone says; each man's service is what his record shows. Where this site tells you a man was Black, or white, or served in a particular unit, it is because a document says so, and the document is cited on his page. [8]

## What this site is

Each stone has a page. It gives a short life of the man, the records that life rests on, and what is not known about him. About fifty of the three hundred have been identified from their stones and from the regimental rolls; a handful have been researched in the National Archives. [2] The rest are listed by what their stones say. The work is continuing, and the pages will change as it does.

The lot is cared for by the City of Providence, Department of Parks, North Burial Ground. The research is by the volunteers named on each page. Corrections are welcome.
